01 — What the original actually does

The rules engine

Verified by decoding the shipped main.js. Where the published description is vague or incomplete, the code wins; a few behaviours below are not documented anywhere.

Board and generation

A W×H grid of 16px cells. The board is generated on the first click, not at load. Every cell except the one clicked enters a candidate pool; each tier's quota is then drawn at random. Two consequences matter:

  • First-click safety covers exactly one cell. You can open a "7" on move one with no cascade. Modern Minesweeper normally guarantees a zero-opening.
  • Placement is pure random — there is no solvability check. Unresolvable 50/50s exist and ship.

Numbers are sums, not counts

A revealed cell shows the sum of its eight neighbours' tier levels. A 4 is one tier-4 creature, or two tier-2s, or a 1+3, or four 1s. You never learn how many neighbours are creatures. This is the single biggest departure from Minesweeper and it is where all the deduction depth lives.

Cascade fires only when a cell's number is 0, recursing into all eight neighbours. Since 0 implies no creature neighbours, a cascade can never pop one.

Combat

You always strike first. Enemy HP, enemy attack and EXP value are all derived from one number — its tier. Your attack is your level.

enemy HPEyour attackL
enemy attackEEXP on kill2^(E−1)

For a fight you survive, the closed form is damage = E × (⌈E/L⌉ − 1). The shape to internalise: one tier up always costs exactly E HP while E ≤ 2L. Two tiers up is where it cliffs.

LV 0 is a death sentence, by construction

At level 0 your attack is 0, so enemy HP never decreases and the loop runs until you die. That is exactly how BLIND mode forbids combat — it doesn't special-case anything, it just sets LV 0 and HP 1.

Progression

EXP is cumulative and never resets. Thresholds live in a per-mode array; past its end the game returns 9999 + ex, which is why a maxed level displays NE9999.

There is no healing anywhere in the original

Max HP is read exactly once — at the win screen, to decide whether you earned a perfect clear. HP only ever goes down. This makes HP a guess budget, not a combat resource: you spend it on 50/50s and misreads, never on required fights. It is the most load-bearing fact in the design.

Creature Sweeper keeps this within a board and refills between them — so the guess budget is per-board rather than per-run.

Win, lose, and marking

  • Battle types — win when every creature is dead. You must click every creature cell; empty cells may be left unopened.
  • Search types (BLIND) — win when every non-creature cell is open. Creatures are unkillable.
  • Lose — HP hits 0; all creatures are revealed.

Number keys 0–9 mark the cell under the cursor. Two behaviours are worth stealing outright:

  • Marks are a click-guard. If a cell's mark exceeds your current level, the click is blocked entirely. Marking a cell "5" at LV2 makes it physically unclickable — intent and protection in one gesture. This is the game's only safety rail and it is genuinely good design.
  • In search modes only, marking decrements that tier's remaining counter, so the LV row behaves like Minesweeper's flag counter. In battle modes the counter moves only on an actual kill.

Smaller details: no chording (clicking an opened empty cell does nothing); clicking an already-defeated creature toggles its sprite against its own neighbour-sum, so killing something grants an extra reading; the timer starts on first click and caps at 9999s.

03 — The finding

The tuning identity

Let Ck be the total EXP obtainable from every creature of tier ≤ k. Compare that against each mode's level thresholds:

The upper thresholds are exactly Ck, in every single mode. That is not coincidence — it is the game's core tuning rule:

The rule

The last N level-ups each require killing literally every creature at or below that tier. Earlier level-ups are granted partway through a tier (roughly 20–70% of the way), then the curve locks to 100%.

What follows from it

  • You cannot out-level the content. You reach max level at the precise instant only the top tier remains. HUGE has exactly one tier-9 creature, and you hit LV9 the moment it is the last thing on the board — a deliberate boss beat hidden in an EXP table.
  • Every mode is perfect-clearable with zero damage. Verified arithmetically for all five battle modes: playing strictly in tier order, there are always enough same-or-lower-tier creatures to fund each level-up. Damage is never mechanically required.
  • Lock depth is the real difficulty dial. EASY, NORMAL and EXTREME lock the top two thresholds; HUGE locks four; HUGE × EXTREME locks five. More locked tiers means less slack. It scales cleanly and is a far better progression knob than board size alone.

Lock depth amplifies the need for solvable generation

A deep lock means you must positively identify every creature in a tier before you can advance. If the last one sits in an unresolvable position, you are stuck and forced to gamble. Deep locks are only fair on top of guaranteed-solvable boards — and measured with a complete deducer (section 10), not one board 10 of EXTREME, HUGE × EXTREME or ORACLE in thirty was guess-free — HUGE × EXTREME's has since been retuned so it can be won.

04 — Creature Sweeper

Progression mode

Each game type owns a ladder of 10 boards of increasing difficulty, and each type scales along its own axis so the ladders feel different rather than merely longer.

The model

  • Ten boards per type. Board 1 is the original mode's tuning (or very near it); board 10 is the ceiling.
  • You climb in order. A type opens at board 1; clearing board n unlocks board n+1.
  • Unlocks are permanent. Every board you have reached stays selectable — after a loss, after quitting, across sessions. Losing costs you the attempt, never the progress.
  • Beating board 10 clears the type, unlocking the next type and that type's Full Run.
  • Boards 11+ exist and are playable, but are not required for anything.
  • HP refills between boards. Each board is its own self-contained budget — except in a Full Run, which is the one mode where it does not.

What a save has to hold

Per type: the highest board unlocked, whether board 10 has been cleared, and the Full Run history. Per board: cleared, and cleared-without-damage — the original already distinguishes a clear from a perfect clear, and it costs nothing to keep. A run keeps how deep the best attempt reached as well as whether one was completed, because a mode that takes an hour has to have something to show for an attempt that ended on board 7.

Ladder handoff

Each ladder's top board is tuned to sit roughly where the next ladder's bottom board sits, so a player who finishes EASY 10 meets NORMAL 1 as a lateral step rather than a cliff. EASY 10 is a larger board than NORMAL 1 but a sparser one — longer, not sharper.

Unlock graph

Two kinds of gate, and they make different claims.

  • A type gate — "clear NORMAL" — is a statement about readiness: this ladder teaches something the next one assumes. Only three ladders still use one: NORMAL after EASY, and the two combined Normal ladders, HUGE × EXTREME and HUGE × BLIND, which require both parents — a ladder that is two ladders at once should not be reachable without having played the things it combines. A combined type gating this way also leaves the board-count schedule alone, which is the one place in the unlock data that can strand a save.
  • A board-count gate — "clear 25 boards, anywhere" — is a statement about time served, and it is what every other ladder uses. They do not teach each other: a hex grid teaches you nothing about a torus, and neither teaches you Sudoku, so chaining them was always a fiction — and an expensive one, because it made a player who wanted the ragged cave grind three shapes they had no interest in to get there. Counting boards lets them arrive from whatever direction they like.
  • The menu has four categories: Normal (the original game's seven modes), Shape, Magic and Special. From 15 boards, every five opens the next ladder in each category that has one left, so a step offers a choice of what kind of thing to play next (decision 0036).

The thresholds run —. The order is set by hand to pace what the player meets, not by difficulty: DUNGEON, the second easiest by the honest player's measure, closes Magic, after every spell has been met. BLIND waits one step past the rest. Each menu column is ordered to match, so it reads in the order a player will meet it. Every board counts once, scaling boards included.

A board-count gate can deadlock the game, and silently

A type gate that cannot be met is a cycle, and a cycle is obvious. A board-count gate is just a number, and nothing about it says whether a player can reach that number without the type it guards. Set BLIND's threshold above what the type-gated ladders offer and the only way to unlock BLIND would be to have already unlocked BLIND — a save that is simply stuck, with nothing to point at.

So the budget is asserted rather than assumed. The types that gate on type-clears alone — EASY and NORMAL — offer 20 tuned boards, past the first step at 15, and every step after that opens at least ten boards for the five it asks, so each gate can be met by playing what the last one opened. A test walks the graph from an empty save and fails if any type is left stranded, and a second one walks the counted gates in order on tuned boards alone — never a scaling board — so a player who never goes past board 10 still reaches everything.

Why BLIND is off the critical path

A player who bounces off pure deduction can still finish the game, and a player who loves it gets a second game to start after the first one ends. BLIND opens one step after every other counted ladder; bringing it forward is one number (BLIND_AFTER_STEPS in ladders.py).

Built — Full Run

All ten boards of a type back to back on one HP pool, unlocked by clearing board 10 of that type, so a run is never how a player first meets a board. The pool is board 1's HP and the per-board schedule is ignored; board 1 is the most generous entry in every schedule, so the ceiling never drops under the player mid-run. After each board you clear you heal half the pool, rounded down, capped at the pool.

Level and EXP reset on every board, and that is forced rather than chosen. Thresholds are Ck of a specific board, so carrying level forward would start board 2 near its ceiling and every board after the first would be free. Only HP carries. Mana does not either: a run-long pool would make the late boards the ones where spells are free, which is backwards, and it would reward not casting in a game whose measured problem with spells is that nobody is ever forced to spend.

None of this touches the zero-damage guarantee, which is a statement about one board: at level k every tier ≤ k is a free kill and the gate to k+1 is at most Ck. Because a run resets the level economy every board, that argument still applies ten boards deep — so a whole run is clearable without the heal ever mattering, which is what npm run sim:run asserts across every type. HP stays a guess budget; the run just makes it one budget instead of ten.

The rounding down is load-bearing on exactly one ladder. BLIND's pool is 1, so its heal is 0 and a run there is a single-mistake run. Rounding up would restore it in full and turn the mode into ten unrelated boards on that ladder — the harsher rule is the only one that means anything at a pool of one.

Boards beyond 10 — built

Every schedule below is linear, so boards 11+ continue it by its own average step per board, clamped: density at 34% (battle) and 30% (search), board size at 64×32, lock depth at T−1, HP at the floor the tuned ladder itself chose. They are emitted as a separate extended list, not folded into the ten — the ladder is still ten boards, a Full Run is still ten boards, and clearing a type is still board 10. Only the scaling picker reads past it.

Three rules make the continuation honest rather than merely long. A cap is never set below where the ladder already finished — HIVE ends at 35% and ARCANE at 34.5%, both past the nominal 34% ceiling, and clamping them to it made board 11 sparser than board 10. A flat dial stays flat: EASY's lock depth is 2 by design, and letting it drift to T−1 would make board 30 of EASY a differently-named EXTREME. And a step that produces no new board is skipped, not treated as the end — EXTREME's board grows 0.444 cells at a time, so board 11 rounded to board 10 and a stop-on-repeat gave it no continuation at all despite 30 cells of width left to grow.

Two things can make a later board hold less than an earlier one, and both are refused outright rather than patched. A wider CROSS can be a smaller one, because its arms are |x − cx| ≤ param/2 wide and cx lands on a cell centre for an odd box and between two for an even one — 35 across gives nine-wide arms, 36 gives eight. A board whose Ck went backwards cannot carry the thresholds of the board before it, because the EXP to meet them is no longer on it. Search ladders are exempt, having no level economy at all — BLIND's own tuned ten step C1 backwards at board 6, where the tier count rises and the same creatures spread wider.

A type stops where it stops changing, which lands in very different places: SUDOKU gets three boards and stops at its generator's floor, HUGE × EXTREME runs to 34 now that its retune left it room, and the gentler ladders run to the thirties. Past that a board would be the previous board with a fresh seed, which replaying the previous board already gives you. Every one of the 598 boards is swept by npm run sim and clears at full HP.

How the numbers are derived

Per type, a board index n supplies size, tier count, density, HP, lock depth and an opening-generosity value α₀. Everything else is computed:

  • Creature count M = round(density × W × H), apportioned across tiers by one of two archetype shapes — descending (a linear ramp from T down to round(T/4), which reproduces both the original's 5-tier and 9-tier distributions) or flat. HUGE and HUGE × BLIND additionally pin their top tier to a fixed apex count.
  • Ck from that distribution.
  • The top lock thresholds are set to Ck exactly; the rest to αk · Ck, with α ramping from α₀ up to 0.70.
  • A final pass enforces that no board is easier than the one below it on any threshold.

Sanity check on the generator: fed the original's board sizes and densities, it reproduces HUGE's distribution as 52,46,41,35,30,24,18,13,1 against the shipped 52,46,40,36,30,24,18,13,1, and reproduces HUGE × EXTREME's five locked thresholds exactly.

05 — First move

The opening

The game opens the board for you. Rather than protecting a cell you pick, it finds the zero-region whose cascade reveals the most cells and opens that one before you touch anything.

Worth being precise about what gets revealed: a cascade from a zero cell opens that cell's 8-connected component of zero cells plus the fringe of numbered cells touching it. So "biggest" should mean the largest total reveal — component and fringe together — not the most zeros.

It simplifies the pipeline

There is no first click left to protect, so the board no longer has to be generated lazily on input. It generates at load, in order: distribute creatures, compute numbers, find the best opening, verify the rest is solvable from there, reveal it. The timer then starts on the player's first real click, not on the auto-open.

Does it hold up at high density?

A zero cell needs all eight neighbours empty, so zero-regions thin out as density climbs — exactly where a good opening matters most. I simulated 400 boards for each of the 70 board configurations, 28,000 boards in total:

  • Every board had at least one zero-region. Not one failure in 28,000 — so the degenerate case is real in principle but never hit at these densities.
  • The smallest opening ever produced was 9 cells (EXTREME, boards 7–10): a known-empty cell plus its eight numbered neighbours. Even the worst case is a usable foothold.
  • The rule is self-regulating. Opening size is inversely coupled to density with no tuning at all — generous exactly where the game is teaching, tight exactly where it is hard.

One place it may be too generous

EASY board 1 reveals a median of 104 of 256 cells — 41% of the board, because at 11.7% density the empty cells percolate into one huge connected blob. By NORMAL board 1 that is already down to 10%, and by EXTREME board 10 to 2.8%. For the teaching ladder a large freebie is arguably correct, but if it feels like the board plays itself, the fix is a cap: take the largest region under a ceiling of N% of the board rather than the largest outright.

Fallbacks and knobs

  • If no zero-region exists — impossible at ladder densities, reachable in Free mode — open the single cell with the lowest number instead, breaking ties by most empty neighbours.
  • Guarantee a floor by making "best opening ≥ N cells" a generation constraint and re-rolling if it fails. Generation is cheap; this makes the 9-cell worst case a promise rather than an observation.
  • Always-largest is deterministic per board, which may make openings feel samey. Picking at random among regions within ~80% of the best keeps the quality and restores variety.
  • Expose it — auto-open (default) · guaranteed-zero on a cell you pick · 3×3 protected · one-cell protected (the original) · none.

06 — Built

Magic & mana

The prices tripled, and that is the first time they have mattered

At the old 25/10/50/100, buying your way out of every moment a deductive player is cornered cost between 1% and 32% of a board's whole mana pool. No ladder could run out of mana by playing well, so price was inert everywhere — doubling or halving the table would have changed nothing. At 30/75/150/300 the same shopping list costs 40–86% on the late boards, so you can no longer answer everything and which moments to buy is a decision. Early boards stay cheap (2–30%), because that is where the spells are introduced. Beacon has since come down to 85, priced by measurement against Reveal (its row below).

Measured across fifteen seeds a board: casting fell from 2.4 to 1.4 Reveals a board on DUNGEON while the share of them that unlocked something rose to 87%, which is the shape of a player who has started choosing. Clear rates moved a little and did not collapse — DUNGEON 98→93%, DONUT 91→87%, ORACLE 51→49%, the rest unchanged.

It stays one global table. What varies between ladders is already carried twice over, by income (a board's pool spans 150 to 1,233) and by demand (forced guesses span 0.1 to 6.0 a board); a per-ladder price would be a third axis saying what those two already say, and would stop "Reveal costs 75" being a fact learned once.

Status

Four spells are implemented and playable — Reveal, Census, Exercise and Beacon. ARCANE and ORACLE are built around them; the cut-out ladders — DONUT, CROSS, WRAPPED CROSS, DIAMOND, RAGGED CAVE and PYRAMID — carry ARCANE's loadout as well, and DUNGEON carries that plus Exercise. Echo and Scry below are still on paper. The rest of this section is what the build follows.

Worth naming the underlying thing first: mana and HP are two currencies for the same commodity — certainty. HP buys it by absorbing a wrong guess after the fact; mana buys it in advance by removing the guess. Every spell below is priced against that, and it is why the set stays small.

The economy

Income is a hybrid, and the split is deliberate rather than arbitrary.

  • Kills pay +tier each — linear, where EXP is exponential at 2^(E−1). That difference does the balancing by itself: early you are mana-rich and EXP-poor, late the reverse, so spells are most available exactly when you are weakest and thin out as your level takes over.
  • Exploration pays 1 per 4 empty cells you uncover yourself. A trickle, not a wage.
  • A starting pool of 75, the same on every magic ladder, keeps the opening moves from being spell-less. It is exactly one Reveal, or two Censuses with change: enough to answer the first question the board asks, never enough to answer the second. It tripled with the prices, because what it is for is that one opening answer rather than a particular number.

Why kills lead and exploration only trickles

Paying per cell opened sounds equivalent, and is not. Kill income is exact and known at design time (Σ qᵢ·i) and it scales with difficulty — a denser board holds more creatures and so more mana. Cell income does the opposite. Measured across the ladders, total cells outnumber total kill-mana by 3.7× on the sparsest board and only 0.6× on the densest, so paying per cell at any flat rate makes the easy boards the mana-rich ones.

Per-cell is also cascade-lumpy: one lucky click that opens sixty squares would hand over a windfall, and this design has spent a lot of effort reducing what luck decides.

So the trickle is small, and measured on full clears it lands where intended — a clear minority that shrinks as boards get harder:

A trap worth knowing before any spell is written

Any spell that removes a creature must still grant its full EXP. The locked thresholds are Ck — the total EXP available from tiers at or below k. Kill a creature without collecting its EXP and that total can no longer be reached, so the gate becomes permanently impassable and the run is silently dead. Either every removal pays full EXP, or thresholds have to be recomputed against what is left. The first option is much simpler.

Your second idea should be free, not a spell — and here is the proof

Auto-opening every neighbour of a revealed cell whose number is ≤ your level isn't a gamble at all. It is provably zero-damage. The number is the sum of neighbouring tiers, so no single neighbour can exceed it. If that sum is ≤ your level L, then every adjacent creature has tier E ≤ L, and damage is E×(⌈E/L⌉−1) = 0 for all of them.

So it is this game's version of chording, and it is always correct. Charging mana for a provably free action just taxes tedium. Make it a one-click Sweep — resolve all safe moves — available in every mode, respecting the mark click-guard so it never touches a cell you flagged above your level.

Two refinements the base rule misses

The rule as stated is needlessly timid, because a cell's number counts neighbours you can already see. Both fixes work on the same quantity — the part of the number still hidden.

  • Subtract what is visible. A defeated creature's tier is a fact, not a guess, so a 7 beside a dead tier-6 hides only a 1. This costs nothing: it is still a proof, just a tighter one.
  • Subtract the player's marks. If you marked a neighbour "4" beside a 5, the residual is 1, so everything else in that ring is free at LV1. This reaches dramatically further — but it is an assumption, not a proof. A wrong mark costs HP, and the bound covers only the unmarked neighbours, since the marked ones are what produced it.

The two promises are different, so they are two buttons

Folding marks into Sweep would quietly downgrade it from "cannot cost HP" to "cannot cost HP if your reading is right". Those are different enough that the player should choose, so the prototype ships both: Sweep (S) resolves only what is proven, and Sweep + marks (D) additionally trusts your claims.

Each button carries a live count, and the mark-assisted one enables only when it would open strictly more than the proof can — so it lights up exactly when your marks have bought you something, and stays dark when they have not. A residual that goes negative proves a mark is wrong; that cell is currently skipped, but surfacing the contradiction would be free feedback.

Your first idea splits in two depending on whether it opens or only shows. Scry above is the "show" half. The "open" half — blow a 3×3 and fight whatever is in it — is a fine power spell too, but note it is a genuine gamble rather than a tool: anything above your level still hits you, and it must pay full EXP per the trap above.

Loadouts as identity

Rather than splicing magic into the tuned main line, the two magic types branch off NORMAL as their own chain. Nothing already measured moves, and the branch can be retuned freely. The shaped ladders take the same loadout without taking the same prices: their boards hold fewer cells, so the pools are smaller in absolute terms, but the shape of the economy is ARCANE's — exploration pays 23–25% of income on their first boards falling to 18–20% by their tenth, against ARCANE's 26% and 20%, and the smallest of them still carries thirteen full kits. Costs and the starting pool carry over untouched.

The zero-damage guarantee survives magic untouched

Not one of the four spells removes a creature. That is the whole reason the set looks like this: information, a borrowed level and movement only. Every C_k gate still receives every point of EXP it was tuned against, and the invariant tests pass on both new ladders unchanged.

07 — Proposed

Generation & topology

More distribution shapes

Two archetypes are in use. Four more are worth having, and one of them is barely a variant at all:

  • Ascending — more high tiers than low. Inverts the economy and makes the opening hours brutal.
  • Bimodal — a swarm of tier 1 and a cluster of apex creatures, little in between. A 6 becomes either six tier-1s or one tier-6, which is a much sharper read than the muddle in the middle.
  • Single-tier — every creature the same level. Numbers collapse to count × tier, which is literally Minesweeper with a multiplier. A clean puzzle variant and a good tutorial board.
  • Descending + apex — already in use by HUGE, worth naming as its own shape.

All of them keep the zero-damage guarantee

This generalises further than I expected. At level k you can safely kill every tier ≤ k, which yields Ck EXP, and the threshold to reach k+1 is at most Ck by construction. By induction you can always climb the whole ladder without taking a point of damage — whatever the distribution shape. So new archetypes can be added freely; the invariant is a property of the threshold rule, not of any particular curve.

Placement rules

Six primitives cover your examples and a couple more. Each needs a scatter fallback for leftovers, because Ck depends on placing exactly the quota per tier:

  • Scatter — uniform random. What the original does.
  • Cluster(tier, n, box) — your tier-1 triads in a 3×3.
  • Pair(tierA, tierB, box) — your 2-and-4 pairs in a 2×2. Shipped, in a much stronger form: as a rule over the whole board rather than a sprinkled motif. See PAIRS below.
  • Lair(boss, escort, n, box) — your boss ringed by its tier-below escorts.
  • Spread(tier, minimum distance) — anti-clustering; keeps apex creatures apart.
  • Band(tier → region) — a spatial difficulty gradient: low tiers at the rim, high tiers toward the core.

I simulated each against three real boards — same creature counts every time, only the placement changes — measuring the auto-opening, how much of the board is zero, and the numbers themselves. The measurement is the engine's own now (src/sim/placement.ts): its generator, its neighbour sums, its opening. The strategies that never shipped exist only there, as tier layouts handed to the engine to measure.

Five things fall out, and they are not what I would have guessed:

  • Clustering makes boards easier, substantially. Grouping creatures empties the space between groups: triads plus pairs lift the opening by 29–40% and raise zero-cell counts by a third to a half. If you add clustering, densities have to rise to compensate or every ladder silently gets gentler.
  • Lairs make boards harder, and how much depends on the distribution. On EXTREME's flat curve — 34 top-tier creatures — lairs halve the opening and push the average number from 6.6 to 11.4. On HUGE, which has two apex creatures at that board, it is a non-event. Lair impact scales with how many top-tier creatures exist, which makes it a natural pairing with flat distributions and nothing at all on descending ones.
  • Banding moves difficulty around rather than changing its total. The opening grows 8–43%, because the rim is now sparse — but the maximum number climbs too (16→18, 22→29, 28→32) as the core concentrates. You start at the edge and work inward as you level, which pairs neatly with the tier-order climb the threshold rule already forces. The catch is that the auto-opening becomes predictable: it will nearly always be on the rim.
  • Wrapping the edges grows the opening, by 7–17%, even as it cuts the zero count (52→43 on NORMAL) by giving every rim cell a full ring of neighbours: a zero region that reaches one edge now carries on from the other. The Python version of this table had it 0–4% smaller, and that was a bug — it summed numbers across the seam but flood-filled the opening without crossing it — so for a while this table contradicted the wrap-around measurement below, which had it right. A bigger opening may be part of why WRAPAROUND measures as the gentlest ladder of all.
  • A hex grid is easier on every count — 21–26% more opening and 60–80% more zero cells, because six neighbours make smaller sums (average number 4.1→3.1 on NORMAL). The checkerboard's colours, by contrast, barely move the opening at all: the rule decides which tier stands where, not where creatures stand. Its difficulty lives in deduction.

The grouping rules split cleanly in two. Pairing shrinks the opening — every domino sits alone in a ring of empty ground, which spreads the creatures evenly — while packs and lines grow it, because six-cell groups leave empty ground between them. That is the clustering finding above, measured on rules that shipped: pairing is clustering at the smallest scale there is and anti-clustering at every larger one, and the larger one wins.

Clustering was expected to help solvable generation — more structure, more deducible patterns. A solver now exists (section 10) and the shipped rules bear it out: they are the ladders a complete deducer finds far easier than the honest player does, 68–85% fewer forced guesses against 31–59% elsewhere. The clustering strategies themselves never shipped, so it has not been run on them.

PAIRS — every creature has exactly one creature neighbour ·built

It sounds like a rule about couples and it is really a rule about packing. If every creature has exactly one creature beside it, the occupied cells are adjacent pairs — and no two pairs may touch, because a contact would give the creatures either side of it a second neighbour. So the board is a scattering of dominoes, each alone inside a ring of empty ground.

The consequence that does all the work, and it was free

A creature's neighbours are its partner and nothing else. A cell's number is the sum of its neighbours' tiers. Therefore a creature's own number is its partner's tier, exactly — no arithmetic, no ambiguity, no subtraction. The engine already computed a number for every cell including creatures, and already let a defeated one show it, so the mode's single most valuable deduction fell straight out of machinery built for something else. Kill anything and it names its partner. It shipped shown by default here, on the argument that burying it a click away would bury the point of the rule, went back behind a click toggle by request, and is now not drawn at all: the other ladders show a beaten creature's number on hover, and here a lone digit over a creature read as its own level. Sweep's partner proof and the pencil still read it.

Two proofs come off that, and both are in safeCells rather than left for the player to translate into marks by hand — the lesson Census taught:

  • Partner in reach. A defeated creature whose number is at or below your level has a partner you can kill for free, and every other neighbour is empty ground. The whole ring is free to open.
  • Partner already met. A defeated creature with a defeated creature beside it has found the one creature it is allowed to touch, so everything else around it is blank — at any level. Clearing one pair is worth about seven cells of certainty.

Neither can run away, and the reason is the packing rather than anything about levels: a freed ring holds the partner and otherwise empty ground, because no second domino may touch the first. A trigger clears one pair and stops, and the cascade it sets off kills nothing, so it triggers nothing further. Compare the Sudoku rule, which had to be kept out of safeCells entirely because it could iterate its way to a solved board.

The measurement went the opposite way to the guess

The rule was expected to give the board away — sparse, clustered, big voids, a huge free opening. It does the reverse. The exclusion ring around every pair spreads the creatures evenly, and clustering is what makes a zero-region, so the auto-opening comes out 30–65% smaller than a uniform board of the same density (6.9% against 10.0% at 20.6%), and cells hiding nothing at all drop from 18.8% to 10.7%. Measured over 300 seeds a board, PAIRS has the smallest openings in the game: 6.0% of the board falling to 1.1%, against NORMAL's 9.2% to 4.0%.

That is the clustering finding above with the sign flipped, and the two are consistent: pairing is clustering, at the smallest scale there is, but the no-touching rule is anti-clustering at every larger one, and the larger one wins.

So the board plays tighter than NORMAL at a matched density and then comes apart faster than anything else in the game. Every other ladder grows more ambiguous as it goes; this one grows less.

Its difficulty axis is size, and that was forced rather than chosen

Dominoes that may not touch cannot exceed two cells in every six — 33.3% — and a random lay-down jams far below that, measured at 24.8–25.6% over 200 seeds. The quota has to be landed exactly, because Ck assumed it, so the schedule has to stop where placement is still reliable rather than where the board stops being a puzzle: at 26% it places on every seed within 40 restarts, at 28% on one seed in four. A board two creatures light would not throw — it would simply have its top gate one kill out of reach, on that seed only. Same failure shape as the ragged cave's cell count, and the same answer.

Which leaves four points of density, and inside them density does almost nothing. Measured with the honest player from sim:spells, walking 20% to 25% on a fixed board moved the forced guesses from 0.0 to 1.5 and left the first six boards at 0.0 — a ladder with nothing in it, the same failure ARCANE had before it was retuned. Growing the board across the same span gives 0.2 rising to 2.1, at 100% falling to 83% cleared, which puts it on WRAPPED CROSS's curve (0.1 to 2.7, 84%) and inside the variant family. Deduction here is local, so area is what buys you more places to be cornered. It is the only ladder in the game whose axis is size, and it spans the widest range of any tuned ladder: 480 cells to 1,056.

HP is not a dial here, and it looks like the obvious one

This mode's characteristic gamble is "exactly one of these k cells holds a tier T, the rest are empty", with T read straight off a dead creature's number. So a wrong guess is one known, lethal blow rather than an accumulation — and the whole ladder at HP 12 and at HP 14 clears the same share of every board as at HP 10, to the point. It keeps NORMAL's 10. Lock depth was tried too, on the theory that it throttles the ring proof by holding your level down; it moved the clear rate and barely touched the forced guesses, because the EXP economy gets you there anyway.

DOMINOES — PAIRS, dealt as a full set ·built

Every creature still has exactly one creature beside it, but the pairings are now a double-T domino set: every pair of tiers {a,b} with a ≤ b turns up exactly once, doubles included. It assumes the pairing rule, and it used to be gated on clearing PAIRS for that reason; it is counted now, at 40 boards, which still opens it after PAIRS at 35 — the schedule carries the order without a gate that could strand a save.

The set decides the distribution, so it is the Sudoku situation

A double-T set carries every tier exactly T+1 times — T−1 tiles pairing it with each other tier, plus its own double, which carries two. So quantity is flat by construction and is not a dial at all; ladders.py branches around its own distribution path for this type exactly as it does for Sudoku. That flat curve is EXTREME's whole lever, inherited for free: a tier 6 is as common as a tier 1.

What it hands the player is bookkeeping. One open creature identifies a whole tile, not half of one — its own tier you fought, and its partner's tier is its number. The set is finite and known from the first move, so a tile you have found is one you no longer have to fear: there is exactly one five-double, and once a pair reads 5—5 no other five stands beside a five. That reasoning is left to the player rather than put in safeCells, the same call Sudoku makes, and it is the obvious thing to measure next.

No blanks, and the reason is structural

A [0|x] tile is a creature whose partner is empty ground, which breaks the one-creature-neighbour rule both of PAIRS's Sweep proofs rest on. Worse, a blank half is drawn as ordinary floor with nothing to tell it apart, so a quarter of a double-six set would be tiles the player cannot verify — and [0|0] is invisible in principle. The set bookkeeping is the mode, and a set with holes in it is the one thing it cannot have.

Six tiers on every board, so density is nearly the whole dial

Every board is a double-six set — 21 tiles, seven of each tier — or several copies of one. The tier count never moves, so the ladder is copies of the set and how tightly they are packed. That second one turned out to dominate. With the honest player from sim:spells, four sets on board 10 cleared 88% at 22.5% density and 45% at 25% — the steepest cliff of any ladder measured, because a flat six-tier set makes every forced guess as likely to land on a tier 6 as a tier 1. The set count barely moved it: topping out at three, four or five sets all collapsed at 25%.

So the band runs 18.5% to 23.5%, well under the packing ceiling, which is left to the scaling boards. Over 60 seeds a board: 0.1 forced guesses rising to 2.2, 98% cleared falling to 70% — harder than PAIRS's 83%, which is right for the ladder you reach by clearing it.

Two things measured on the way there are still true. Double-nine does not fit a multi-set ladder at all: at 90 creatures a set, the largest board holds five. And HP is no rescue on a flat curve — a double-eight ladder cleared 53%, 53% and 55% of its board 10 at HP 10, 14 and 18.

Between one set count and the next the creature count cannot move, so the only way to raise density is a smaller board — boards 6 to 8 hold the same 126 creatures on 594 cells shrinking to 561. It is the one ladder in the game that gets harder by getting smaller.

PACKS — PAIRS, grown to groups of six ·built

Creatures travel in packs of six, one of every tier, all touching, and no two packs touch. That is PAIRS's rule with six in place of two — "exactly one creature neighbour" is the same statement as "connected groups of two that may not touch". Like DOMINOES it is counted now, at 50 boards, after PAIRS. "All touch" has to mean connected: the most cells that are all mutually adjacent is four on a square grid and three on hex. Pack shapes are loose — grown a random neighbour at a time, so an L or a snake is as legal as a block.

A pack is a group of creatures standing together, so membership is never hidden

Packs never touch, so a pack is exactly a connected group of creatures and needs no link drawn. A covered cell beside a creature is a packmate or empty ground. A packmate is one of the tiers that pack has not shown yet, so once the strongest missing tier is within your level the whole ring is free — and a pack with all six found is ringed by empty ground at any level, PAIRS's "met its partner" proof grown. Both are one number, missingFrom, and Sweep reads it. Neither runs away: a freed ring holds packmates and blank ground only, so a trigger finishes one pack and stops.

The opposite of PAIRS: packs leave the board open

Pairing spreads creatures evenly and gives the game its smallest openings. Packs of six are big clusters, and clustering is what makes empty ground: on 30x16 at 25% density, cells with nothing around them rise from 9.4% on a uniform board to 23.3%. In the real engine board 1's opening is a median 26% of the board, against NORMAL's 9%. The packing ceiling is not what binds it — six cells have far less rim per creature than two, and 36% lays down on every seed measured — so density is free to be the dial.

First guess was 26–34%, reasoning from the open ground; it cleared 35% of board 10. The flat curve is what that missed — one of every tier per pack makes a tier 6 as common as a tier 1. Measured with the honest player taught the rule, 120 seeds a board, the ladder runs 22.5–31.6%: 0.2 forced guesses rising to 5.2, 99% cleared falling to 70%, DOMINOES's 70% at the top. The guesses run well ahead of the clear rate, which is the CHECKERBOARD and DUNGEON signature a third time: a guess beside a pack is capped by what the pack has not shown, so it is cheaper.

Packs come in sixes, so one pack is 1.25 density points on a 480-cell board, and a schedule held on one size rounded neighbouring boards to the same board. The board grows a row or a column every step for granularity rather than difficulty.

CONGA LINE — PACKS, strung out ·built

Creatures dance in lines of six, one of every tier, and the tier 6 always leads. A line steps up, down, left or right from one dancer to the next and never bunches into a 2x2 block, and no two lines touch. The rest follow in a random order. It is PACKS with a shape and an order imposed, so everything PACKS proves carries over unchanged: a line is still a connected group of one of each tier that nothing else touches, and missingFrom reads it as it reads a pack. It is a counted ladder, at 65 boards.

"No 2x2" and "a true line" are the same rule at this length

Two dancers orthogonally side by side without being next in line would close a loop of orthogonal steps, and a loop of grid cells shorter than eight always contains a 2x2 block. A line of six cannot hold a loop of eight. So forbidding the block is forbidding the shortcut, and that buys an exact fact: every dancer has at most two orthogonal linemates, and the ends have one. Two open creatures orthogonally side by side are consecutive, which is why the board ties them together the way PAIRS ties a pair. Diagonals are not constrained, and cannot be: a line turning a corner puts the cells either side of the corner diagonally together.

The leader rule gives Sweep almost nothing, and the reason is the leader

Three proofs follow from the shape, each good at any level: once the leader has a follower beside it, its other sides are empty; a dancer with two open orthogonal linemates is full; and three open creatures in a 2x2 make the fourth cell empty. Measured with the honest player, over 386 stuck points none of the three ever found a cell the numbers and the pack proof had not. The leader is the tier 6, a player going in tier order kills it last, and by then the pack proof has freed every ring on the board.

What does not wait for the leader is that a line is a path, so the dancers still missing hang off its two ends, within as many orthogonal steps as there are dancers missing. Every covered cell beside a partial line that no such walk reaches is empty ground, which is most of the rim once most of a line is found. PACKS cannot say this. It is beyondReach in congo.ts. It has an answer at 9 of those 386 stuck points and moves the ladder by less than the noise. The mode's difference is in how it reads to a person, not in what Sweep can prove.

Lines have more rim per dancer than a compact pack, so they jam a little sooner: 34% lays down on every seed from 30x16 to 60x30, and 35% starts losing seeds on the largest board. Measured at PACKS's own schedule (22.5–31.6%) with the honest player taught the rule, 60 seeds a board: 0.1 forced guesses rising to 3.8, 98% cleared falling to 68%, level with PACKS's 68% at the top. It ships there.

Wrap-around ·built

Two ladders, RING (a cylinder — left and right joined) and TORUS (every edge joined). Both run NORMAL's exact schedule: same sizes, same densities, same HP, same lock depth. That is deliberate. With every other variable pinned, whatever you feel playing them is the wrap and nothing else.

Corner neighbour counts go 3 → 5 → 8 across the three.

The measurement contradicted itself, which turned out to be the interesting part

At equal density a torus has 16–22% fewer blank cells than a square board — expected, since no cell gets a shortened neighbour list any more. But its opening is bigger (44 → 53 cells at NORMAL's opening density), because blank regions now connect around the seam instead of being cut off by the rim.

So the two obvious metrics point in opposite directions, and neither captures the actual cost. Edges are information. A corner has three neighbours and a rim cell five, and those constrained cells are where a sweeper player gets every cheap foothold — the classic openings are all edge patterns. Wrapping deletes every one of them. That is a deduction effect, and no blank-cell count will ever see it. Which is exactly why both ladders keep NORMAL's numbers: the honest way to price it is to play it.

How it is drawn

A ghost band one cell deep beyond each joined edge, repeating what lies across the seam at reduced opacity, with a dashed line marking where the real board ends. The board reserves room for the band when it sizes itself, or it would be drawn off-canvas and never seen.

Two details that matter more than they sound. Hovering lights every neighbour, so on a wrapped board the highlight visibly jumps to the far side — that teaches the topology in about three seconds, faster than any caption. And the band is clickable: a click on the repeat resolves to the real cell it is showing, because anything drawn as part of the board should behave like part of the board.

Board shapes

One lever sits under all of them. A cell's number is the SUM of its neighbours, so fewer neighbours means fewer unknowns means easier to deduce. Edges and corners are free information, which is exactly why wrapping — which deletes all of them — is the only variant that makes the board harder. Every shape goes the other way.

Parameterise masks in cells, not in fractions of the board

Built — DONUT, CROSS, DIAMOND and RAGGED CAVE

Four shaped ladders ship: a ring, a cross, an inscribed diamond, and a cave carved fresh from every seed. Cut-away cells are absent, not empty — they hold nothing, neighbour nothing, and count toward no win condition.

They carry ARCANE's spells on densities first derived for a spell-less board, so they were measured against ARCANE's curve with the honest player, 60 seeds a board — and only two of the four had to move. ARCANE is cornered 24.0 times over its ten boards and clears 82% on average. DONUT was already harder (41, 85%) and RAGGED CAVE already on it (25, 89%), so both stay; CROSS (13.1, 94%) went up 2.5 density points and DIAMOND (10.9, 95%) 4, to 21.8 / 89% and 20.1 / 84%. CROSS's board 10 lands at 5.0 forced guesses and 63% cleared against ARCANE's 5.1 and 63%. Where forced guesses and clear rate disagree — DONUT is cornered twice as often as ARCANE and clears about as much — it is the cheap-guess finding again: a guess on a rim is a guess you know something about.

The cave was the one that stayed parked, and the obstacle was never the carving. Its silhouette moves with the seed, so its cell count moves too — and every level threshold comes from Ck, which needs the creature quota fixed before the board is built. A mask that came back a few cells light would not throw; the board would simply be mistuned on that seed, with the top gate, which is exactly Ck, sitting a kill out of reach.

The way out was to stop treating the count as an outcome. The count is chosen per board, the generator is required to land on it exactly, and a test holds it to that on every seed. Everything upstream then carries over from the fixed shapes untouched — quota, Ck, thresholds, the zero-damage guarantee. Deriving thresholds at generation time would also have worked, and would have cost the whole one-way flow from ladders.py into the engine.

Wrapping a shape is not wrapping a rectangle — WRAPPED CROSS

"Edges are free information, so joining them is the only variant that makes the game harder" is a claim about a rectangle, where a thin rim surrounds a large interior. A cross is nearly all rim, so joining its arm tips deletes very little of it — and does something much larger instead. CROSS is four dead-end corridors sharing a hub; joining the tips turns them into two loops, so a player stuck at one tip can work in from the other. It is the DUNGEON finding with the sign flipped: what sets the count of forced guesses is how many separate puzzles the board is cut into.

Measured with the honest player, 25 seeds a board, at CROSS's own schedule: 0.0–2.3 forced guesses a board against CROSS's 0.3–2.4, and 92% of board 10 cleared against 80%. So it ships at CROSS's schedule lifted 1.2 density points, which put it back on CROSS's curve — 0.1–2.7 guesses, 84% cleared. When CROSS moved 2.5 points up for its spells this moved with it, to 27.2–34.4%, and was re-measured rather than assumed: 21.7 forced guesses over the ladder against CROSS's 21.8, still on the curve, at the price of harder opening boards.

Two things in the engine only surfaced once a board was shaped and wrapped. A hole filtered out of the neighbours it was handed but not out of the ones it asked for, so adjacency was asymmetric at a hole; and the dashed seam was stroked around the bounding box, which on this board would claim a join across background where there is no board to join. Both are one-line fixes, and both were invisible on every board before this one.

Disconnected shapes break the opening

The auto-opening reveals exactly one region. A mask with separate islands leaves every island but one with no way in, and no amount of deduction fixes it. Either reject disconnected masks at generation, or give each connected component its own opening — which is the more interesting option, since it makes an archipelago board a set of parallel puzzles.

09 — Look and feel

Type identity & temp assets

Every game type gets its own palette, creature set, number face and voice. These are placeholders chosen so that nothing needs drawing or recording yet — but each one occupies a real slot, so replacing it later is a swap rather than a rebuild.

Temp creatures: pips on a 3×3 grid

A tier-N creature is drawn as N pips in a 3×3 grid, die-style. It costs no art, draws procedurally at any size, covers exactly the 1–9 range the game needs, and stays countable rather than memorised — useful while the numbers are still moving. It also echoes the 3×3 neighbourhood the whole game is built on, which is a happy accident rather than a design goal.

Colour and shape carry different jobs, which is what made the first attempt unreadable. Pip shape carries type identity — each ladder has its own. Pip colour carries tier identity and is global, so a tier-4 creature looks the same on every board.

Five hues cover tiers 1–5; tiers 6–9 reuse the same five wearing a gold halo. The hues are spread rather than ramped warm-to-hot: a yellow-orange-red ramp put adjacent tiers at ΔE 2.5 under protanopia, which is precisely why the glyphs were hard to read at a glance. Since the pip count already encodes magnitude, colour is free to do identity instead. The one soft pair that remains — yellow against green, ΔE 6.4 protan — is disambiguated completely by the count.

Number faces and voices

Number faces are placeholder stacks — the point is that the slot exists per type and the board immediately looks different, not that these are the final faces. For audio, the cheapest thing that produces seven distinct identities is one shared event set rendered through seven voice profiles: same MML strings, different waveform and base octave. No audio files at all.

Where the gameplay identities are thin

Four types already play differently in a way a player would name out loud: EXTREME's flat distribution means a high tier is as likely as a low one, so no number is ever safely assumed; HUGE is a long campaign with nine tiers and a single apex creature you reach max level exactly in time to meet; HUGE × EXTREME is the wall; BLIND removes combat entirely.

EASY and NORMAL are currently the same game at different sizes — same descending distribution, same five tiers, same HP, differing only in board and density. That is the one real gap. The cheapest fix that makes EASY mechanically distinct rather than merely smaller is to run it at four tiers instead of five: it caps the worst number at 32 instead of 40, shortens the level curve, and makes it a genuinely gentler ruleset rather than a smaller board. It would mean regenerating EASY's ladder, which is one line in the schedule.

10 — Everything else

Free mode knobs

Each of these is hardcoded in the original. Each is a parameter you could expose. Red shows the original's fixed value.

The QOL gaps

Confirmed absent from the original: guaranteed-solvable boards, meaningful first-click protection, chording, undo, board seeds or sharing, custom sizes, save and resume, colourblind options (marks are green-only, creature numbers red-only), any stats beyond a share string, and any difficulty rung between EASY's 30 creatures and NORMAL's 99.

The two highest-value fixes are the ones that make the original feel unfair rather than hard: solvable-board generation and proper first-click protection. The mark click-guard is already good design — build on it rather than replace it.

Solvable generation, measured

There is a complete deducer now (src/sim/solver.ts). It finds every covered cell that every layout consistent with the screen makes free, and it reads only what a player can see: the numbers, the tiers already open, the placement rules beside open creatures, and the per-tier counts the palette shows. Whatever it leaves out can only make it find fewer free cells, so what it finds is a floor. Played beside the honest player on the same seeds — 30 a board, every battle ladder, with no layout it could not fit and no HP ever lost on a cell it called free — it says three things.

Every forced-guess figure on this page is an upper bound, by about two

At 57–97% of the honest player's stuck points there was a free move it missed — ORACLE board 3 at LV2: a 3 and a 9 each leave a cell possibly a 3, but a 3 there would leave the 7 beside it needing a tier 7 on a six-tier board. A perfect deducer is cornered 31–59% less on the plain and shaped ladders and 68–85% less on the placement-rule ladders: CHECKERBOARD falls from 1.21 forced guesses a board to 0.18. Those rules combine across numbers in ways a one-cell reading never reaches, so those five ladders play easier than their tuning says.

Generate-and-test is affordable early and impossible late

A board generate-and-test would keep is one a perfect deducer finishes without once being cornered. That is 94% of NORMAL's boards, 73% of ARCANE's (37% of its board 10s), 36% of DONUT's (3%) — and not one board 10 of EXTREME, HUGE × EXTREME or ORACLE in thirty (HUGE × EXTREME before its retune). The top of the hard ladders needs construction, placing creatures so the deduction exists, or a weaker target: no forced guess that can kill, rather than none at all, which is what HP as a guess budget already implies.

ORACLE 7–10 are decided by guesses, whoever plays them

A perfect deducer clears 90–100% of ORACLE's boards 1–6 and 23%, 3%, 0% and 3% of boards 7–10, cornered 6.7–9.1 times a board. The cliff is between boards 6 and 7. It is not the honest player being weak; it is the boards.

The weaker target rescues most of what the strict one cannot

"No forced guess can kill" asks less than "no forced guess": whenever deduction runs out, some cell within reach must be proven below the tier that would end the board at the player's current HP. A board that meets it cannot be lost by a perfect player. Guessing the cell with the lowest proven worst case, 30 seeds a board, the share of board 10s that meet it is ARCANE 97% (against 37% guess-free), DONUT 73% (3%), EXTREME 7% (0%) — and 0% on HUGE × EXTREME (before its retune) and ORACLE, where the problem is the schedule, not the generator.

The cliffs are the lock

Lock depth maxes out at one less than the tier count, where every threshold above the first is a full-tier-clear gate. EXTREME reaches it at board 9 and ORACLE at board 7, and each ladder falls off a cliff on exactly that board: a max-depth gate needs every creature of a tier found before the next level, and the last one of a tier is often the one in a 50/50. Held one short of the maximum, nothing else changed, a perfect player clears ORACLE 7–10 at 97 / 63 / 40 / 47% instead of 23 / 3 / 0 / 3%, and EXTREME 9–10 at 60 / 60% instead of 20 / 7%. Two more HP on ORACLE barely moves it; three points less density helps a little. Two short of the maximum was measured too and is not worth it: nothing more on EXTREME, and on ORACLE the same gain as one short plus two HP — while dropping the top boards' lock below the middle of their own ladder.

HUGE × EXTREME has a winnable board 10 now

It was deep all the way up — lock 5 to 8 of 9 — and a perfect player cleared none of boards 8–10. Keeping the maximum lock would have needed density below board 1's. So the lock stops at 7 on boards 7–10 and density steps back at board 7 to pay for that step. A perfect player now clears boards 5–10 at 75 / 70 / 65 / 55 / 65 / 60% (was 70 / 60 / 25 / 0 / 0 / 0), and the honest player wins one board 10 in ten. It is still the hardest board in the game; it is no longer a coin-flip marathon nobody finishes.

11 — Built

Creature Sudoku

Status

Built and in the ladder: ten boards, generated guess-free, tuned on the givens count and verified by npm run sim:sudoku. The player supplies the Sudoku reasoning; Sweep harvests the cells they have identified.

A 9×9 board where the tiers obey Sudoku's rules: each of the nine digits appears exactly once in every row, column and 3×3 box. The digits are tiers 0–8, not 1–9 — tier 0 is empty ground, and treating it as a digit like any other is what makes the whole thing fit.

That gives a second deduction channel running alongside the neighbour sums, and a global one: Sudoku constrains cells nowhere near anything you have revealed, which nothing else in the game does. Every other variant here is local.

Digits 0–8 is what makes the opening free

Because tier 0 is a digit, every row, column and box contains exactly one empty cell — nine of them, spread by construction. Those nine cells are the opening. They are safe by definition, they show their numbers, and they grant no EXP, so nothing is invented and the auto-opening needs no new case at all.

Why 1–9 does not work, and it is not a small thing

At 100% density there is no empty ground, so anything the board pre-reveals is a pre-killed creature — and a removed creature must grant its full EXP or the Ck gates become unreachable. Thirty random pre-kills grant roughly 1,700 EXP, past C7, handing the player level 8 before their first click. The only safe form is pre-killing whole low tiers, since clearing every tier ≤ k grants exactly Ck, which is exactly the LV k+1 gate. That works, but 0–8 removes the problem instead of managing it.

The measurement: do the two channels compose?

That is the question the idea lives or dies on — whether the sums add anything once Sudoku is in the room, or whether this is Sudoku wearing a hat. Played by an honest solver that sees only what a player can see (Sudoku singles and hidden singles, plus the neighbour-sum bound) and guesses when it runs dry. 60 boards per row, opening fixed at the nine empty cells, every gate a full-tier clear:

The neighbour-sum channel is worth about fifteen givens. A board that plays fair at 24 givens would need around 38 to be a fair Sudoku. So the majority of the information in a playable board comes from the sweeper side, and the answer is that they compose — this is a hybrid rather than a reskin.

Difficulty is the givens, and almost only the givens

Because generation rejects anything that needs a guess, the clear rate is 100% by construction and measuring it proves only that the generator worked. What difficulty actually means here is the shape of the deduction — and the honest measure of it is the tightest round: the fewest cells any single round of deduce-kill-deduce ever hands you. A board that opens in big sweeps is a different game from one that yields three cells at a time.

The floor is generation cost, not playability

Build cost is flat to about 18 givens, 15× at 16, 45× at 14, and there is no guess-free board at all below about 11. The ladder stops at 15 because the cost curve turns over there, not because the boards stop being interesting. Below the floor the generator throws rather than quietly shipping a board it cannot vouch for — the same alarm the cave's cell count has, and for the same reason.

One consequence worth stating plainly: because boards are tested against a fixed solver strength, fewer givens does not demand harder techniques. It makes such boards rarer and their chains longer and tighter. That is a real difficulty axis but a bounded one, and it is the thing to revisit first if the ladder reads as flat.

Lock depth is a mild second dial, running 3 to 7 across the ladder.

Note what is gone. Density, tier count and distribution archetype are all fixed by the Sudoku rule — quantity is nine of each tier on every board, so Ck is identical on every board of the ladder. The three axes every existing ladder scales on do not exist here, and ladders.py's density schedule and distribution machinery are bypassed entirely. This needs its own code path, not another row in TYPES.

The size axis is gone too: 9×9 is 9×9. A 4×4 and a 6×6 with 2×3 boxes give a teaching ramp, but 16×16 breaks the EXP curve at 215 a kill.

The real argument for building it

HP stops working as a guess budget. Damage is E·(⌈E/L⌉−1), so a tier-8 at LV1 costs 56 against this ladder's HP of 14–20 — one wrong guess is fatal several times over. So this type must be guess-free, and it is the one type that easily can be. Generate-and-test against the propagator is cheap enough to do on every board, which makes SUDOKU the first ladder in the game with a proven zero-forced-guess guarantee — and a live testbed for the solvable-generation work the rest of the game still wants.

What it cost

  • Marks became candidate sets. mark is one value per cell, and you cannot solve a Sudoku with one pencil mark per cell. Cell.notes is now a bitmask of candidate tiers, bit t for tier t — and deliberately not a second mark, because a mark is a value that feeds the neighbour-sum subtraction and a set cannot. A QOL win for the whole game, not just this type.
  • Notes can protect you; they must never expose you. That asymmetry was learned the hard way. A set is read in exactly one direction — if even the smallest candidate is above your level then every possibility is, and the cell is guarded. It is deliberately not read the other way. Shipped briefly, a cell whose candidates were all at or below your level counted as safe, and Sweep would open it: a cell pencilled "2 or 3" at LV5, actually a tier 7, offered up for 7 damage. A pencil mark means "the tiers I have not ruled out yet", which is the opposite of a claim about what the cell is, so acting on it permissively charges the player for thinking out loud. When you are sure, you commit the pencil to a mark — pencil for candidates, pen for a digit you have solved.
  • Sweep must stay weaker than the generator's own solver — and that is the one thing this type does not get for free. Everywhere else a board contains real ambiguity, so any automatic deducer runs out on its own. These boards are made by rejecting anything the propagator cannot finish, so the moment Sweep was taught the Sudoku rule it cleared 100% of every board in a single click. It did, briefly, and it made the ladder unplayable.
  • So Sweep became a harvester instead. It opens every cell whose tier you have written down and which your level already covers — the clues under the strict button, your own marks under the assisted one, keeping the proof-versus-claim line exactly where it is everywhere else. The division of labour is: you do the Sudoku, it does the clicking.
  • The neighbour-sum proof is not weak here, it is empty. Measured at zero cells on every board of the ladder, even with half the grid correctly marked: hidden sums run to sixty at this density, so hidden ≤ level never holds and the mark-assisted residual never comes near a level either. Both buttons were permanently dark, which is what the harvest rule replaced — it gave up nothing.
  • It still cannot run away with the board, because marks are player-authored and nothing regenerates them: you only ever get back the work you put in. That is the same property that keeps notes safe, and any future convenience that fills marks or candidates automatically would hand it straight back.
  • Clues had to become unerasable. Re-marking toggles a mark off, so a player could rub out a given they could never get back and turn a guess-free board into an unfair one. Givens carry a given flag and refuse to be marked or pencilled over.
  • Census is fully inert. It counts creatures among the neighbours, and here that is eight minus the empties — which the Sudoku rule already tells you. ARCANE's loadout does not transfer, and this ladder ships without magic; the natural replacement, if it ever gets any, is unit-scoped: naming the cell in a row or box that holds a given tier.
  • The boxes needed making visible at all. A row and a column announce themselves; a 3×3 box is nine cells that look exactly like their neighbours, so the constraint the board is built on was invisible. Two things now carry it. Alternate boxes take a translucent wash — a wash rather than a second set of colours, so it derives from whatever theme is in play and lands the same on covered tile and cleared floor. That second part is what matters: by the endgame the whole board is floor, which is precisely when the grouping is hardest to hold. And the boundaries take a heavy rule, drawn as one pass over the finished board rather than per cell, so no cell can paint over its neighbour's half of a shared edge. It has to be around twice the weight of an ordinary cell edge or it simply reads as one more of them — at equal weight it was invisible.
  • A dealt clue and a claim you made are different things, so they are different colours. Givens are gold, the player's own marks stay green. Gold also reads as fixed, which is honest: a given is the one annotation on the board you are not allowed to change.

What the numbers do not cover

The solver does singles and hidden singles only — no pairs, triples or X-wing — and the sum bound ignores Sudoku interaction among a cell's unknown neighbours. So it is a floor for a strong player and a ceiling for a weak one. Treat the givens band as ±3 and re-measure through the real engine rather than a second implementation of the board rules, which is the lesson design/opening.py already taught once.

12 — Still open

Decisions pending

01

Is half the pool the right Full Run heal?

The knob this section used to ask for now exists, and it is set to half the pool per cleared board — which gives a NORMAL run an effective budget well above its ten HP while still leaving one bad board felt several boards later. It is fair at any setting, because zero-damage play is always possible and the simulation confirms a run needs no heal at all; but fair and enjoyable are different questions and only playtesting settles which fraction feels right. Both ends are one number away: a heal of zero is the original Ironman, a full heal makes a run ten unrelated boards with a shared clock.

02

When should the BLIND ladders actually unlock?

Settled (decision 0036): BLIND opens at 50 boards cleared, one step after every other counted ladder, and HUGE × BLIND once HUGE and BLIND are both cleared. That keeps them off the critical path while asking nothing but play.

03

Do the search ladders need a fourth scaling axis?

BLIND and HUGE × BLIND have no level economy, so they scale on only three axes — size, density and tier count — and cannot use lock depth at all. They run out of headroom sooner than the battle ladders. If they feel thin around board 7, the options are a fourth axis (a move limit, a timer, a shrinking mark allowance) or simply accepting that they are shorter ladders. Lower stakes now that they are post-game content.

04

Should a Full Run record its boards on the ladder?

Settled by the unlock rather than by preference: a run opens only once board 10 is cleared, so every board in it has already been cleared and there is no progress a run could add. A failed run therefore costs only the run, which is the right price for opt-in post-clear content. What is still worth deciding is whether a board cleared inside a run should be allowed to set that board's best time — it currently cannot, on the grounds that clearing board 6 at 2 HP carried in from board 5 is not the same claim as clearing board 6 outright.

05

Should the placement-rule ladders be re-derived against a stronger player?

CHECKERBOARD, PAIRS, DOMINOES, PACKS and CONGA LINE were tuned against the honest player, and a complete deducer finds 68–85% of their forced guesses avoidable against 31–59% everywhere else — their rules give far more than a one-cell reading takes. A real player sits somewhere between the two. Either re-derive those five against the solver, which would make them denser, or accept that they reward understanding the rule more than other ladders do, which is arguably the point of them.

06

What should the hard ladders' top boards do about forced guesses?

No board 10 of EXTREME, HUGE × EXTREME or ORACLE was guess-free under a perfect deducer in thirty seeds, so generate-and-test cannot fix them, and ORACLE's boards 7–10 are decided by guesses outright. Measured since, the answer splits by ladder. HUGE × EXTREME is settled: its board 10 has to be winnable, and it now is, with lock held at 7 and density stepped back (60% for a perfect player). For EXTREME and ORACLE the cliff is the step to maximum lock depth, and holding the top boards one lock short turns them from unwinnable into hard (ORACLE 7–10 at 97–40% for a perfect player, EXTREME 9–10 at 60%). Two short was measured and is not advised. Still to decide: apply one short to EXTREME 9–10 and ORACLE 7–10, and whether ORACLE also gets two more HP on those boards, which lifts its board 10 from 47% to 63% for a perfect player.

07

Should marks be refused the way pencil marks now are?

The pencil will not write a candidate the placement rule has already ruled out — an odd tier on a light CHECKERBOARD square, anything but empty ground or the partner's tier beside a defeated PAIRS creature. A mark still will. A mark is a claim, and the mark guard locks a cell on it, so refusing one stops the player being wrong in a way the board can already see: arguably a kindness, arguably the game playing itself.

08

Should DUNGEON be more puzzling, or deadlier, or neither?

It sits well under ARCANE at the top — 98% of board 10 cleared against 63%. More rooms make it more puzzling without making it more dangerous: at 7, 9 and 11 rooms a deductive player is cornered 17.8, 21.0 and 25.6 times over the ladder (ARCANE 24.0) and still clears 98%, 96% and 95% on average (ARCANE 82%), because a doorway lets you look into a room before you step into it. So eleven rooms would match ARCANE's puzzle; matching its danger would take less HP. Left as it is for now, to be settled by playtesting rather than by simulation.
